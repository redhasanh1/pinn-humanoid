Physics-informed neural networks

every demo runs live in your browser

A neural network normally learns from examples alone. A physics-informed neural network (PINN) is also scored on whether its answer obeys the laws of physics.

loss = data error + λ · physics error

The physics check runs at thousands of points where no measurement exists, so the network learns the rule behind the data, not only the data.

Why training gets easier

Training means rolling downhill on a surface of possible answers until reaching the lowest point.

With data alone, that surface is full of dents: many answers fit the few measurements equally well, and training can settle in the wrong one.

Adding the physics term smooths it into one clear valley. The only answers that score well are the physically possible ones, so training finds the right one, fast.

The network inside

Everything the robot knows is stored in the connections between these nodes. Each connection holds a number, and learning adjusts millions of them.

  1. In: camera images, joint positions, the instruction.
  2. Middle: layers that turn pixels into "ball, 1.8 m away, drifting left".
  3. Out: the next moves for shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers.

The pulses are one decision flowing through. The models we run have about 3 billion connections.

Heavy inference, fast reflexes

BrainA 3-billion-parameter robot model (NVIDIA GR00T N1.7, π0.5) on a cloud GPU plans the next 2 seconds. About once a second.
CheckerA large vision-language model catches mistakes mid-motion, running on Cerebras inference. Several times a second.
ReflexLearned skills on the robot: intercept, cushion, release. 50 times a second.
MotorsServos hold each joint at its target. Every 20 ms.

Where this is going

Robots that learn overnight. Skills practised a hundred thousand times in simulation, in wind and from every angle, before they touch the real world.

Physics as the teacher. Physics-informed models let a low-cost, 3D-printed robot predict how things move, from a handful of real examples.

Thinking at the speed of motion. Checking its own plan many times a second, a robot can catch a slip mid-grasp and fix it before anything falls.

The robot

A 3D-printed humanoid with five-finger hands that cooks, tidies and fetches, and one day walks the dog. It rolls on a wheeled base while its walking legs are trained in simulation. It learns its skills in physics simulation before trying them for real, and we'll be using Cerebras's fast inference for its thinking. Built by students in Toronto.
